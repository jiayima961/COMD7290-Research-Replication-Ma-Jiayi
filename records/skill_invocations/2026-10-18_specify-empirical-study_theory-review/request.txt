The study analyzes civility in mediated public discourse about the public role of religion in six democracies (Australia, Germany, Lebanon, Switzerland, Turkey, USA), across newspapers, news websites, and political blogs. It measures outrage at two levels: (1) the holistic “tone” of a news item and (2) explicit actor-to-actor references; recognition is measured only at the actor-reference level.

1. Political system (majoritarian vs. mixed vs. consensus)
At the news-item level, outrage was not highest in “pure” majoritarian systems as hypothesized. Instead, mixed systems (Germany and Turkey) showed more outrage than consensus systems (OR = 1.49), while majoritarian systems showed less outrage than consensus systems (OR = 0.54). The abstract summarizes this as mediated discourse being heavier on outrage in mixed political systems than in “purely” majoritarian and consensus systems.
At the actor-reference level, political system had no statistically significant effect on either outrage or recognition.
Recognition was descriptively highest in Lebanon and Switzerland (consensus systems) and lowest in Australia and the USA (majoritarian systems), but these differences were not statistically significant. Overall, the study concludes that civility did not vary systematically across political-system types in its six-country sample; future research should disaggregate which features of political-system “packages” matter.

2. Socio-cultural divides (contested secularism vs. stable secularism)
At the news-item level, countries with contested secularism contained more outrage than countries with stable secular consensus (OR = 1.69). Thus deep socio-cultural division was associated with more outrage in mediated discourse.
At the actor-reference level, the socio-cultural divide did not significantly predict outrage.
Contrary to the expectation that divided societies would show less recognition, actor references in contested-secularism countries were more likely to contain explicit recognition (OR = 2.20). The authors interpret this as divided societies producing more intense mediated exchanges overall—both outrage and recognition—because group relations and identities are more salient. In less divided societies, recognition may be less necessary because commonality is more tacitly presumed.
Overall: deeply divided countries contained more outrage but also more recognition than less divided countries.

3. Media types (top-down vs. bottom-up news production)
Blogs (bottom-up, non/semi-professional political blogs) were consistently the most outrage-prone media type. At the item level, news websites (OR = 0.17) and newspapers (OR = 0.28) were significantly less likely than blogs to contain outrage. At the actor-reference level, news websites (OR = 0.23) and newspapers (OR = 0.34) were also significantly less likely than blogs to contain explicit outrage.
Blogs also featured the lowest proportion of explicit recognition in actor references. Newspapers and news websites were significantly more likely than blogs to contain recognition (news websites OR = 1.99; newspapers OR = 3.28).
Thus, top-down professional journalism (newspapers and news websites) mitigated outrage and fostered recognition compared with bottom-up political blogs. The authors argue this supports the continued deliberative value of traditional journalism.

Key cross-level caveat: Most effects were concentrated in the lower outrage categories (“no outrage” vs. “slight outrage”); moderate-to-very-strong outrage was rare overall. The study also cautions that its country sample is small and focused only on the religion-in-public-life issue.

TWO MAIN THEORIES UNDERLYING THE STUDY

1. Deliberative democratic theory
The study’s normative framework comes from deliberative democracy. Deliberative theory provides a relatively clear concept of civility and treats civility as an important criterion for evaluating the democratic quality of public debate. The authors do not collapse civility into a single holistic concept; instead, they operationalize two normative extremes: outrage as the negative extreme and recognition as the positive extreme. Outrage is a communication style aimed at provoking visceral responses through overgeneralization, sensationalism, misleading or inaccurate information, ad hominem attacks, and partial truths about opponents. It violates speech-related, relational, process, and modality norms and is generally harmful for public discourse because it blurs criticism of ideas and criticism of persons and can undermine foundational social respect. Recognition is the positive counterpart: a communication style that treats others as beings with particular needs, with equal respect and autonomy, and as unique contributors to society. From the deliberative perspective, recognition is generally valuable for democratic public discourse, including recognition toward outgroups and ingroups.

2. Recognition theory (Honneth)
The positive concept of recognition is grounded in Honneth’s argument about the importance of recognition for democratic public discourse. The study follows Honneth in treating recognition as a rich normative concept, rather than merely tolerance or politeness. Recognition means respecting others as legitimate participants and as beings with equal autonomy and unique contributions. The authors argue that recognition is valuable in public discourse and may empower ingroup members and serve as a role model for broader forms of recognition. This recognition-theoretic framing complements the deliberative framework by giving the “positive side” of civility a theoretical foundation.
