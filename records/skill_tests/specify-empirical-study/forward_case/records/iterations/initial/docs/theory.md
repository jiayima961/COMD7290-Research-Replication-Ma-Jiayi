## Phenomenon, gap and contribution

**Authors' documented argument.** Civility matters to the democratic quality of mediated debate, yet definitions and operationalizations differ. A literature centered on negative communication in particular countries or media leaves systemic determinants and the positive side of civility less understood. Löb, Rinke, Weinmann and Wessler integrate two normative concepts: outrage as a potentially harmful communication style and recognition as explicitly respectful address. Their contribution applies and integrates deliberative, institutional, cleavage and news-production explanations in a comparative contingency account; it does not establish a new causal process experimentally. [Article](../sources/local/Lob_et_al_2024.pdf), PDF pp.2–6 / printed pp.274–278; contribution and limitations pp.16–18 / 288–290.

**Concepts.** Outrage uses rhetorical tactics intended to arouse strong responses; an emotional message is not automatically outrageous. Recognition conveys respect, legitimacy or appreciation, including toward an ingroup; it does not require agreement. The absence of coded outrage does not establish recognition, and vice versa. An item may contain both kinds of reference even though the codebook treats the two as mutually exclusive within the same reference. Item tone is a holistic ordinal judgment; reference outcomes are explicit directed acts. [Article](../sources/local/Lob_et_al_2024.pdf), pp.275–276, 278–279; [codebook 08](../sources/local/codebook_08_actor_reference.pdf), §§8.6–8.8, PDF pp.7–8 (and recognition note at p.7).

**AI synthesis.** Institutional compromise, group segmentation and newsroom constraints are proposed explanations linking context to communication. The models observe categorical proxies for the contexts and the communication outcomes. They do not measure psychological anger, identity salience, individual economic motivation, accommodation or gatekeeping as mediators. The arrows in Figure 1 express the specified predictive model, not identified causal effects.

## Derivations before evaluation of published outcomes

The six derivations use the theory passages at printed pp.276–278. Reading the source in page batches exposed the opening results at pp.281–282 before drafting; this test therefore cannot claim blinded prospective derivation. The predictions below are fixed by the authors' theory wording, irrespective of the later results.

1. **H1a:** Consensus institutions require accommodation; majoritarian institutions permit political dissociation. If this institutional style extends into media discourse, majoritarian contexts should contain more outrage. Targets: M1 tone and M2 reference outrage, majoritarian versus consensus, positive coefficient.
2. **H1b:** Compromise requires treating others as legitimate discussion partners. Recognition should therefore be greater under consensus institutions. With consensus as base, M3 majoritarian versus consensus has a negative coefficient.
3. **H2a:** Contested secularism heightens salient group conflict; parties' group-defense appeals and social distance should fuel outrage. Targets: M1 and M2 contested versus stable secularism, positive coefficients.
4. **H2b:** The same division should weaken constructive engagement and public commitments to compromise. Target: M3 contested versus stable secularism, negative coefficient. Higher recognition is a reported contrary result, not the original prediction.
5. **H3a:** Professional gatekeeping and civic norms should restrain attention-seeking outrageous communication. Compare websites with blogs and newspapers with blogs separately in M1 and M2; each coefficient should be negative.
6. **H3b:** Organizational oversight and professional respect norms should encourage explicit recognition. The website and newspaper coefficients in M3 should each be positive relative to blogs.

The mixed-system dummy is needed in the fitted model but has no separate directional author hypothesis. Website versus newspaper comparisons are also exploratory; they must not replace the two professional-media versus blog contrasts. See hypothesis_matrix.csv for 12 distinct directional targets and separate coverage rows.

## Scope and competing explanations

The empirical domain is selected leading newspapers, news websites and political blogs discussing the public role of religion in Australia, Germany, Lebanon, Switzerland, Turkey and the USA, August 2015–July 2016. It does not directly represent all communication, social media, audience effects, all topics or other periods. Each political-system by divide cell contains one country; country-specific history, language, events, media composition, topic intensity and genre may confound the associations. Events such as the 2016 US campaign and July 2016 events in Turkey may affect the comparison. These are documented limitations (article pp.289–290), not additional fitted controls.

**Methodological criticism.** Country categories are not randomly assigned and six contexts provide weak separation of country and system explanations. Rotated double coding and adjudication improve data quality but do not prove the absence of systematic cross-country error. Different item and reference patterns may reflect aggregation, selection into explicit actor references, or containment of quoted outrage rather than contradictory measurement. Ingroup/outgroup recognition is not distinguished in these models. Non-significance is compatible with limited precision, low event prevalence, measurement or model limitations; it does not disprove the proposed mechanism. A significant contextual coefficient likewise does not verify an unmeasured process.
